# Sources (checked 20 Nov 2026, Malaysia time)
The 1910 book itself could not be fetched (Project Gutenberg refuses automated readers), so Furness's words come from Milton Friedman's essay, which quotes the two passages at length, and are cross-checked against a peer-reviewed paper and other sources below.

- Jo Lindsay Walton, "Bitcoin and stone money: Anglophone use of Yapese economic cultures, 1910–2020", Finance and Society 8(1) (2022) 42–66, DOI 10.2218/finsoc.7126 — read at https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/BE26078E3B8A244832D550D70B9380C3/S2059599900001138a.pdf/bitcoin_and_stone_money_anglophone_use_of_yapese_economic_cultures_19102020.pdf
  - In 1910 William Henry Furness III published The Island of Stone Money, "based largely on his two-month stay on Yap seven years prior" (p. 44).
  - Quotes Furness 1910 (pp. 96–99) on the stone lost when a raft was cut adrift in a storm: "the stone sank out of sight" (p. 44); German colonial authorities "collected fines by marking stones with black paint rather than physically moving them" (p. 44), to extract labour "for road works" (p. 46).
  - Stones "cut in quarries on other islands, principally Palau, over 450 kilometers Southwest", brought "via canoes and rafts, later using Western ships"; voyages "lengthy and dangerous"; sizes "from a few inches to about twelve feet" (p. 43). Stones "remain in use" today (p. 43).
  - Critical: writers from the Global North "have misrepresented Yap"; Furness forced rai into the idea of currency and left out gift exchange (pp. 42–45); the stories were retold by Keynes, Friedman and textbooks. Gillilland: stones were left for "fishing equipment, canoes, for pigs or for a feast" (p. 54); "no evidence that rai stones were ever the sole money used on the island" (p. 43).
- Milton Friedman, "The Island of Stone Money" (Hoover Institution Working Papers in Economics E-91-3, 1991; ch. 1 of Money Mischief, 1992), reproduced at https://makingfriendsandenemies.blogspot.com/2010/09/island-of-stone-money.html — quoting Furness 1910:
  - the family whose wealth was unquestioned, "and yet no one, not even the family itself, had ever laid eye or hand on this wealth", "lying at the bottom of the sea" for "two or three generations"; an ancestor "secured this remarkably large and exceedingly valuable stone, which was placed on a raft to be towed homeward"; "A violent storm arose, and the party, to save their lives, were obliged to cut the raft adrift"; "they all testified that the fei was of magnificent proportions and of extraordinary quality"; "universally conceded ... that the mere accident of its loss overboard was too trifling to mention"; "The purchasing power of that stone remains, therefore, as valid".
  - "it is not necessary for its owner to reduce it to possession"; "the coin remains undisturbed on the former owner's premises"; fei "ranging in diameter from a foot to twelve feet".
  - the paths: "the chiefs of the several districts were told that they must have them repaired"; "the command, which still remained unheeded"; "impose a fine for disobedience on the chiefs of the districts"; an officer "marked a certain number of the most valuable fei with a cross in black paint to show that the stones were claimed by the government"; the people "turned to and repaired the highways"; "Then the government dispatched its agents and erased the crosses"; "resumed possession of their capital stock, and rolled in wealth".
  - Friedman: "From 1899 to 1919 the Caroline Islands ... were a German colony"; Furness (1903) "spent several months on the island". Furness himself dates the purchase from Spain "1898".
- NPR Planet Money transcript, 10 Dec 2010: https://www.npr.org/transcripts/131963928 — reads Furness aloud: "This instantly worked like a charm. The people, thus dolefully impoverished, turned to and repaired the highways"; Germany took over "around 1900"; Palau "about 250 miles south, southwest of Yap".
- Wikipedia, "William Henry Furness III": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Furness_III — American physician and ethnographer from Philadelphia (1866–1920); "He returned to the South Pacific in 1903, and spent two months among the Wa'ab people on the island of Yap."
- eHRAF World Cultures (Yale), Furness 1910 record: https://ehrafworldcultures.yale.edu/cultures/or22/documents/010 — field date 1903, "a doctor's visit to Yap in 1903" (abstract only; full text paywalled).

Debated points the script attributes: both stories are Furness's account ("a doctor heard", "A friend told him", "the doctor wrote"); the footnote says anthropologists find Western retellings simplified. The crosses are as Furness describes them (Walton n. 9); a blog post (moneyness.ca, 2013) says a later study recorded painted initials instead — not checked, so the picture follows Furness. German rule: the chip says "from 1899" (Friedman; the German–Spanish treaty), Furness wrote 1898. Distance to Palau differs by source (250 miles to 450 km; Furness "some 400 miles"), so the video gives no number. Stones were not the only money, so the script says "Big payments were made in stone wheels" (canoes, pigs, feasts per Gillilland). The 12 ft stamp: 12 ft ≈ 3.7 m. The CTA "old sailors' legend" is invented folklore, labelled as such, and not attributed to the people of Yap.
