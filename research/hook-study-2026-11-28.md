## How it was done, and what it cannot tell us
- 10 hook tutorials: two YouTube searches (100 results), ranked by likes, the ten most-liked ones that teach hooks (nine transcripts read, one Short watched). Likes as shown on 28 Nov 2026.
- 20 shorts: vidIQ's Instagram + TikTok search for English money-story, economy and trading posts with 3M+ views (143 posts, all from Aug–Nov 2026, the window its index covers) and its YouTube Shorts list with 5M+ views; from those, the most-viewed ones that tell or explain something, plus the biggest trading posts. Watched scene by scene with vidIQ's video reader (its walkthrough, not our own eyes). Two TikToks had gone private or were under review and were replaced.
- Cost: 280 vidIQ credits (Keith approved about 285; apart from the 20-a-day story budget).
- Limits: these are winners only. The study shows what they share; it cannot show that a video without these things would have failed. View counts also depend on how many followers an account has. That is why every finding went into the hook lab as a level or a starting guess to be tested on our own Reels, and nowhere as a law.

## The formula
The first 10 seconds
1. Key visual first. Choose the one picture that proves the story before writing a word; frame 0 shows it or how it begins, already moving. (T8, T4; W3, W14, W1)
2. A headline that stays. 12 of the 20 keep a headline or labels on screen from frame 0 to the end, word-by-word captions under it. 132 of the 143 have text on screen in the first 3 seconds. (W5, W6, W10, W13, W15 …)
3. The first words are the hook, at 0.0 s. Of the 16 with a voice, 14 speak in the first second, and the first words are the hook itself. Short sentences. No date, no name, no greeting. (T3 "delay", T4, T6)
4. One exact question in the viewer's head. The shapes the top videos use:
   - the viewer's own question, asked out loud ("Why is this bag €2,000?", W1)
   - a what-if with "you" ("If you put $10,000 in a safe today…", W4; W3, W5)
   - a huge number with people or "you" attached ("3 Million People Just Lost Access To Their Money", W12; W11, W15)
   - an unnamed someone who made a fortune just before the news (W13)
   - an object that is the number (a 20-billion-dollar note held to the lens, W14)
   - a clash before any explanation ("Shut that laptop!", W6)
   - a headline that contradicts what people believe (W10)
5. Why me, inside 15 seconds: one line that turns it onto the viewer ("What that means for you is…", W11; W12 works out what the viewer would lose above the insured amount). (T3 "irrelevance")
6. Tease, don't pay. Name what is coming and hold it ("we are not even close to the largest one", W14; W5 announces one catch before saying what it is). The hook's question is answered in the second half. (T1, T5)
7. A new picture about every 1.5 s in the first 10 s (1.25–1.4 s in six of the edited ones; T4 reports 1.4–1.6 s for the biggest YouTubers), then every 2–3 s; a sound on every change. (T7, T9; W3, W4, W10)
8. Picture, headline, first words and sound say the same thing. Test: the first 5 s with the sound off must still tell what this is about. (T8, T4)

The middle
9. Act it before you explain it: a scene with a line or two of dialogue (W1, W6, W7, W8, W19) or an analogy with "you" in it (W3, W4, W5), then the facts.
10. A turn every 10–15 s: a line that asks whether the viewer thinks this is a joke, then "So where did all this money come from?" (W7); an announcement that the crazy part is still coming (W14); the good news, then the scary part (W15). (T2)
11. A ladder with one everyday thing tracked: a coffee at $3, $8, $430 (W5); 20 billion, 50 billion, 100 trillion (W14). Each step quicker.
12. One proof picture at the reveal: a stamp after one stitch (W1), three coins dropped into a hand (W3), a jug of new money poured into a glass (W4), a counter falling from a billion to 48,000 (W5).
13. A dry joke is fine (W7, W15, W19).

The end
14. End on the point. 12 of the 20 have no CTA at all; most stop within a few seconds of the payoff.
15. If comments are wanted, ask the video's own question, answerable in one word or number ("So… where would you stop?", W5; "Take a wild guess who it was.", W13), or ask about the viewer's own life (W4: how much their costs have risen). Two of the 20 ask for a keyword (W10 in a comment, W11 in a DM) and still reached millions.
16. Make one thing worth sending to one person: a line (W14's last line), a picture of two paths (W2), a truth about the viewer's own life (W20).

## Notes on each tutorial (T) and each short (W)
Our own words throughout; at most one short line quoted from each video. vidIQ credits: two YouTube searches 10 · nine transcripts 45 · three finance searches 15 · 21 watches 210 (two more failed and were refunded) = 280.

### The 10 hook tutorials
T1 Logan Smith, "How to make shorts that go viral every time" (3.12M views, 129,556 likes, 9:41, 18 Jun 2024)
- The algorithm is not luck: it pushes what holds people. Topic first (what this audience already watches).
- The mistake he shows: paying off the topic 5 seconds in, so nothing is left to wait for. The answer goes at the end.
- His "HPC": Hook (first 5 s: introduce the topic and leave questions open; fast, no pauses, music, captions) → Progression (do what the hook promised, step by step, with problems on the way) → Climax (the promise fulfilled, last).
- His model hook is one sentence stating an absurd goal (a snowball fight in a desert), which makes the viewer ask how.
- Posting: four a day was too many for him (about half got picked up, quality dropped); one a day was his sweet spot. One creator's experience, not data.

T2 Logan Smith, "How to get retention SO HIGH your shorts always go viral" (383k views, 16,866 likes, 11:16, 23 Jul 2024)
- The retention line decides everything: make it flat. Hook → storytelling → payoff.
- Hook: short, easy, says the idea AND names a payoff, under 5 s. A text hook plus a picture that explains the situation can be enough.
- Voiceover stories: put conflict in the script (things go wrong, then get solved); words like "but" and "however" keep people waiting. Tension is the main tool.
- The payoff must match what the hook promised.
- Editing: as fast as possible, only the strongest moments; the music changes with each section and its build-up makes tension; sound effects on moments.
- He also recommends comment bait (a deliberate mistake, a planted comment from a second account). Not for us: deceptive.

T3 Kallaway, "Give me 15 mins, and I'll make your hooks impossible to skip" (1.22M views, 52,433 likes, 15:49, 3 Jul 2025)
- A hook has one job: the viewer opts in. It needs topic clarity + on-target curiosity.
- Four mistakes: delay (the topic must be clear in the first 1–2 s; a vague teaser gives no context; the drop is steepest in the first 2 s) · confusion (fewer and simpler words, active voice, only one way to read it) · irrelevance (say you/your, not I/me; a pain the viewer already has) · disinterest (open a curiosity loop with contrast = the distance between what the viewer believes and the alternative).
- Order: topic clarity in sentence 1, contrast in the next one or two. A hook is 2–3 lines.

T4 Aprilynne Alter, "how to make a killer youtube intro" (1.75M views, 85,474 likes, 11:32, 8 Sep 2023; long-form intros)
- The intro has one job: meet and beat what the title and thumbnail promised. First sentence = the title, first shot = the thumbnail.
- Then a curiosity gap, the context needed, and "input bias" (show how much went into it).
- Show, don't tell: the intro must work with the sound off. Front-load the stimulus: by her count MrBeast changes the picture every 1.4 s in his intros, Mark Rober every 1.6 s.
- Test: watch it with no sound and in a noisy place; wherever you look away, cut.

T5 heyDominik, "I Studied 1,000 Hooks, Here's How to ACTUALLY Go Viral" (881k views, 24,887 likes, 20:07, 7 Jul 2025)
- People swipe on autopilot; the first frame and line must make the brain stop. Open a loop, keep it open as long as possible, and when it closes open another.
- The most common fault is no context. Put it as text at the top inside the safe zone, small and clean, on an uncluttered frame with one clear subject.
- Challenging a common belief is one of the strongest hooks, and the first frame itself should show the contrast.
- The hook must plant one exact question. A text hook opens the gap instead of naming the thing ("This coffee should not taste good…"); hide the answer in frame 1 and give it at the end.
- A person on a journey gives the emotional connection that pictures alone don't.

T6 Kallaway, "How to Create Irresistible Hooks" (906k views, 34,344 likes, 16:35, 19 Dec 2024)
- Three steps: a context lean-in (the topic at once + a reason to lean in: common ground, a benefit or pain, a metaphor, or something mind-blowing) → a scroll-stop interjection ("But get this:") → a contrarian snapback that goes the other way.
- Visual hooks matter far more than spoken ones (his claim): 3–5 words of big bold title text + the most compelling picture with just enough motion + the spoken line, all three together.
- Lead with the benefit or the pain. Wrap the unknown in something known (a brand, a celebrity, a film) for instant common ground.
- Speed to value: about 4 s before a short is lost; the first hit of value in or right after the hook. Hook sentences short, longer later.

T7 DecodingYT, "How to Make VIRAL YouTube Shorts in 2026" (833k views, 16,163 likes, 15:27, 28 Aug 2026; Hindi-English)
- Two numbers decide a Short: swipe-away rate (most viral Shorts: 30% or less, often under 20%) and average percentage viewed (viral Shorts: close to 100% or above through rewatches; aim for 80%+). His claim, no data shown. Ours: 35–42% leave in 3 s and the average viewer sees 13–48% of the video.
- Hook = stop them in 2–3 s AND give a real reason to watch to the end. Ten formats: the climax hook (the most striking picture, cut just before the climax), contrarian, "[audience], stop scrolling", fear, authority, time-sensitive ("in 2026"), an abstract concept with a name, "Did you know", "If you want X, this is for you", a fixed-number list.
- Editing: a new picture every 3–4 s, a sound effect on every change, movement at the very start as a pattern interrupt, captions (many watch muted), text out of the UI zones.
- Topics: low friction (understood in 30–60 s without effort), a strong curiosity gap AND a payoff; proven formats: comparison, challenge, experiment, reaction.

T8 Kallaway, "I Studied 100 Viral Hooks, These 6 Will Make You Go Viral" (333k views, 16,145 likes, 21:56, 19 Mar 2025)
- A hook exists to open a curiosity loop; contrast opens it; about 5 s to make it stick.
- Six formats: fortune teller (this is the future of…), experimenter (a demo, friend to friend), teacher (lessons from…), magician (a visual or sound stun, stacks with the others), investigator (a secret most people don't know), contrarian (what you believe and most people don't). Any topic can take any of the six: pick the one with the biggest contrast.
- Four hooks at once: spoken, visual, text, audio. They must say the same thing. Viewers go picture → sound → back to the picture, so the key visual matters most: choose the first 3–5 s picture first, then write the words to it. No strong picture: make one or drop the idea.
- His own flop: abstract words over pictures that did not show them.

T9 DecodingYT, "How to Create a Killer Intro Hook (Impossible to Skip)!" (416k views, 15,802 likes, 14:36, 17 Jan 2026; Hindi-English; long-form intros)
- Five steps: meet what the title and thumbnail promised in the first seconds → context (why this matters, only as much as needed) → curiosity (a question that cannot be answered without watching; several teasers = several gaps) → a differentiator (why this video and not another: authority, effort or money put in) → sound design (the music sets the feeling; every item on screen gets its own sound).
- The order of the middle three is free; never promise more than the video gives.

T10 Alex Price-Hunt, "Steal these 5 visual hooks" (Short, 16 s, 1.71M views, 35,918 likes, 18 Jan 2025; watched)
- quick appearance (starts mid-motion) · fast cuts (0.2–0.3 s each, a foley sound on every cut) · walk-in (a blurred wide shot snaps into focus while the subject comes toward the camera) · transition hook (a bizarre image + a stomp that shakes the screen) · match cut (the same lines carry over into the next scene).
- For a 3D world: start mid-move; a fast push-in from blur; something heavy lands and the frame shakes; one shape turning into another.

W5 lowpolylands, "HOW MANY TIMES WOULD YOU PRESS IT?" · Instagram 3.7M (62× median) · 34 s
- LOW-POLY 3D like ours: a white mannequin at a desk with a big red button; a top banner with the question; a live scoreboard from frame 0 (presses, money made, the price of a coffee); word-by-word captions.
