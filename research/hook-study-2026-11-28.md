## How it was done, and what it cannot tell us
- 10 hook tutorials: two YouTube searches (100 results), ranked by likes, the ten most-liked ones that teach hooks (nine transcripts read, one Short watched). Likes as shown on 28 Nov 2026.
- 20 shorts: vidIQ's Instagram + TikTok search for English money-story, economy and trading posts with 3M+ views (143 posts, all from Aug–Nov 2026, the window its index covers) and its YouTube Shorts list with 5M+ views; from those, the most-viewed ones that tell or explain something, plus the biggest trading posts. Watched scene by scene with vidIQ's video reader (its walkthrough, not our own eyes). Two TikToks had gone private or were under review and were replaced.
- Cost: 280 vidIQ credits (Keith approved about 285; apart from the 20-a-day story budget).
- Limits: these are winners only. The study shows what they share; it cannot show that a video without these things would have failed. View counts also depend on how many followers an account has. That is why every finding went into the hook lab as a level or a starting guess to be tested on our own Reels, and nowhere as a law.

## The formula
The first 10 seconds
1. Key visual first. Choose the one picture that proves the story before writing a word; frame 0 shows it or how it begins, already moving. (T8, T4; W3, W14, W1)
2. A headline that stays. 12 of the 20 keep a headline or labels on screen from frame 0 to the end, word-by-word captions under it. 132 of the 143 have text on screen in the first 3 seconds. (W5, W6, W10, W13, W15 …)
3. The first words are the hook, at 0.0 s. Of the 16 with a voice, 14 speak in the first second, and the first words are the hook itself. Short sentences. No date, no name, no greeting. (T3 "delay", T4, T6)
4. One exact question in the viewer's head. The shapes the top videos use:
   - the viewer's own question, asked out loud ("Why is this bag €2,000?", W1)
   - a what-if with "you" ("If you put $10,000 in a safe today…", W4; W3, W5)
   - a huge number with people or "you" attached ("3 Million People Just Lost Access To Their Money", W12; W11, W15)
   - an unnamed someone ("Someone bought three million dollars of…", W13)
   - an object that is the number (a 20-billion-dollar note held to the lens, W14)
   - a clash before any explanation ("Shut that laptop!", W6)
   - a headline that contradicts what people believe (W10)
5. Why me, inside 15 seconds: one line that turns it onto the viewer ("What that means for you is…", W11; "if you had a million dollars in the bank…", W12). (T3 "irrelevance")
6. Tease, don't pay. Name what is coming and hold it ("we are not even close to the largest one", W14; "There's one catch.", W5). The hook's question is answered in the second half. (T1, T5)
7. A new picture about every 1.5 s in the first 10 s (1.25–1.4 s in six of the edited ones; T4 reports 1.4–1.6 s for the biggest YouTubers), then every 2–3 s; a sound on every change. (T7, T9; W3, W4, W10)
8. Picture, headline, first words and sound say the same thing. Test: the first 5 s with the sound off must still tell what this is about. (T8, T4)

The middle
9. Act it before you explain it: a scene with a line or two of dialogue (W1, W6, W7, W8, W19) or an analogy with "you" in it (W3, W4, W5), then the facts.
10. A turn every 10–15 s: "Think this is a joke?" "So where did all this money come from?" "And here's the crazy part." "the good news… the scary part…". (T2; W7, W14, W15)
11. A ladder with one everyday thing tracked: a coffee at $3, $8, $430 (W5); 20 billion, 50 billion, 100 trillion (W14). Each step quicker.
12. One proof picture at the reveal: a stamp after one stitch (W1), three coins dropped into a hand (W3), a jug of new money poured into a glass (W4), a counter falling from a billion to 48,000 (W5).
13. A dry joke is fine (W7, W15, W19).

The end
14. End on the point. 12 of the 20 have no CTA at all; most stop within a few seconds of the payoff.
15. If comments are wanted, ask the video's own question, answerable in one word or number ("So… where would you stop?", W5; "Take a wild guess who it was.", W13), or ask about the viewer's own life (W4). Two of the 20 ask for a keyword (W10 in a comment, W11 in a DM) and still reached millions.
16. Make one thing worth sending to one person: a line (W14's last line), a picture of two paths (W2), a truth about the viewer's own life (W20).

### T1 Logan Smith, "How to make shorts that go viral every time" (3.12M views, 129,556 likes, 9:41, 18 Jun 2024)
- The algorithm is not luck: it pushes what holds people. Topic first (what this audience already watches).
- Biggest mistake shown: paying off the topic too early (the secret told 5 s in, nothing left to wait for). The answer goes at the end.
- HPC: Hook (first 5 s: introduce the topic and leave questions open; fast, no pauses, music, captions) → Progression (do what the hook promised, step by step, with problems on the way) → Climax (the promise fulfilled, at the end).
- Example hook (70M views): "I want to be the first person to have a snowball fight in the desert." It makes the viewer ask how.
- Posting: 4 a day was too many for him (only about half got picked up, quality dropped); once a day was his sweet spot. One creator's experience, not data.

### T2 Logan Smith, "How to get retention SO HIGH your shorts always go viral" (383k views, 16,866 likes, 11:16, 23 Jul 2024)
- The retention line decides everything: make it flat. Hook — storytelling — payoff.
- Hook: short, easy, says the idea AND names a payoff, under 5 s. A text hook plus a picture that explains the situation can be enough.
- Voiceover stories: put conflict in the script (things go wrong, then get solved); "but" and "however" keep people waiting. Tension is the main tool: every beat builds toward the payoff.
- The payoff must match what the hook promised (never "it was a tie").
- Editing: as fast as possible, only the strongest moments. Music changes with each section; a build-up in the music makes tension; sound effects on moments.
- Comment bait he recommends (a deliberate mistake; a fake "the ending was crazy" comment from a second account): NOT for us (deceptive).

### T3 Kallaway, "Give me 15 mins, and I'll make your hooks impossible to skip" (1.22M views, 52,433 likes, 15:49, 3 Jul 2025)
- A hook has one job: the viewer opts in. It needs topic clarity + on-target curiosity.
- Four mistakes: (1) delay: the topic must be clear in the first 1–2 s; vague teasers ("the craziest thing I've seen") give no context; the drop is steepest in the first 2 s. (2) confusion: fewer and simpler words, active voice, only one way to read it. (3) irrelevance: "you/your", not "I/me"; a pain the viewer already has (need-to-have, not nice-to-have). (4) disinterest: open a curiosity loop with contrast = distance between what the viewer believes (A) and the alternative (B); stated or implied.
- Order: topic clarity in sentence 1, contrast in the next 1–2 sentences. A hook is 2–3 lines.
- transcripts: TOHQXdCAo5k 5, Xqy9L-88wcw 5, LmXpbP7dD48 5 (running total 40)

### T4 Aprilynne Alter, "how to make a killer youtube intro" (1.75M views, 85,474 likes, 11:32, 8 Sep 2023; long-form intros)
- The intro has one job: meet and beat what the title and thumbnail promised. First sentence = the title, first shot = the thumbnail (Mark Rober: an egg above Earth, "This is space, and this is an egg, moments before I attempted the world's highest egg drop").
- Then: a curiosity gap (will he make it?), the context needed, and "input bias" (show how much went into it: 3 years, the hardest video he made).
- Show, don't tell: the intro must work with the sound off. Front-load the stimulus: MrBeast changes the picture every 1.4 s in his intros, Mark Rober every 1.6 s.
- Test: watch it with no sound and in a noisy place; wherever you look away, cut.
- For us: cover + on-screen text are our "title and thumbnail"; sentence 1 must say what the text says; a new picture about every 1.5 s in the first 10 s (we do 2–3 s).

### T5 heyDominik, "I Studied 1,000 Hooks, Here's How to ACTUALLY Go Viral" (881k views, 24,887 likes, 20:07, 7 Jul 2025)
- People swipe on autopilot; the first frame + line must make the brain go "hm, interesting". Open a loop, keep it open as long as possible, and when it closes open another.
- Most common fault: no context. Put the context as text at the top inside the safe zone, small and clean, on an uncluttered frame with one clear subject.
- Challenging a common belief is one of the strongest hooks ("Lean at 45 and I eat carbs every day"); the first frame itself should show the contrast (the lean woman AND the bowl of carbs).
- The hook must plant one exact question. "Who can relate?" plants none.
- Text hook: open the gap, don't name the thing ("This coffee should not taste good…" beats "vanilla blueberry iced latte"); hide or blur the answer in frame 1; give it only at the end.
- A person on a journey ("my photos 10 years ago vs now") gives the emotional connection that pictures alone don't.

### T6 Kallaway, "How to Create Irresistible Hooks" (906k views, 34,344 likes, 16:35, 19 Dec 2024)
- Three steps: (1) context lean-in: the topic at once + a reason to lean in (common ground, a benefit or pain, a metaphor, or something mind-blowing): "The tech in the Vegas Sphere is insane. Biggest screen ever built, 20 times bigger than an IMAX." (2) scroll-stop interjection: "But get this:" (3) contrarian snapback, the opposite direction: "the screen is the least impressive part… the most impressive part is the audio."
- The same shape for a mortgage video: three mistakes → the average person pays an extra $X → "most people think it's interest rates, but it has nothing to do with that".
- Visual hooks matter far more than spoken ones (his claim: "100 times"): 3–5 words of big bold title text + the most compelling picture with "just enough motion" + the spoken line, all three together.
- Lead with the benefit or the pain. "Cult hopping": wrap the unknown in something known (a brand, a celebrity, a movie) for instant common ground.
- Speed to value: about 4 s before a short "explodes"; the first hit of value in or right after the hook. Hook sentences staccato (short), longer later.
- transcripts: VUT6HhqKkBA 5, xnOe8aA9Pmw 5 (running total 50)

### T7 DecodingYT, "How to Make VIRAL YouTube Shorts in 2026" (833k views, 16,163 likes, 15:27, 28 Aug 2026; Hindi-English)
- Two numbers decide a Short: swipe-away rate (most viral Shorts: 30% or less, often under 20%) and average percentage viewed (viral Shorts: close to 100% or above through rewatches; aim for 80%+ to start). His claim, no data shown. Ours: 35–42% leave in 3 s and the average viewer sees 13–48% of the video.
- Hook = stop them in 2–3 s AND give a real reason to watch to the end. Ten formats: climax hook (show the most striking picture and cut just before the climax: "Can a magnet destroy your smartphone?"), contrarian, "[audience], stop scrolling", fear, authority ("I listened to 500 podcasts"), time-sensitive ("in 2026"), abstract concept ("This is the halo effect, and it's secretly influencing your thoughts"), "Did you know", "If you want X, this is for you", a fixed-number list.
- Editing: a new picture every 3–4 s, a sound effect on every change, movement at the very start (zoom or transition) as a pattern interrupt, captions (many watch muted), text out of the UI zones.
- Topics: low friction (understood in 30–60 s without effort), a strong curiosity gap AND a payoff; proven formats: comparison ($1 vs $100), challenge, experiment, reaction.

### T8 Kallaway, "I Studied 100 Viral Hooks, These 6 Will Make You Go Viral" (333k views, 16,145 likes, 21:56, 19 Mar 2025)
- A hook exists to open a curiosity loop; contrast (A believed, B shown) opens it; about 5 s to make it stick.
- Six formats: fortune teller ("This is the future of…"), experimenter (a demo, friend to friend), teacher ("3 things you can learn from…"), magician (a visual or sound stun: "check this out", a snap, a "visual pacifier" that keeps the eyes busy; stacks with the others), investigator (a secret most people don't know: "This is a secret Japanese city at the base of Mount Fuji, but it's not just any city"), contrarian (say what you believe and most people don't). Any topic can take any of the six: pick the one with the biggest contrast.
- Four hooks at once: spoken, visual, text, audio. They must say the same thing. Viewers go picture → sound → back to the picture to confirm, so the KEY VISUAL matters most: choose the first 3–5 s picture first, then write the words to it. No strong picture: make one (motion graphics) or drop the idea.
- His failed video: abstract words ("generative world model", "future of storytelling") with pictures that did not show them = comprehension loss.
- Order of work: key visual → the fact with the biggest contrast → spoken hook (2–4 lines) → on-screen text that supports the picture → watch it muted.
- transcript TQ7kuoNcni0 5, watch m8J7t1JXCLA 10 (running total 65)

### T9 DecodingYT, "How to Create a Killer Intro Hook (Impossible to Skip)!" (416k views, 15,802 likes, 14:36, 17 Jan 2026; Hindi-English; long-form intros)
- Five steps: (1) meet what the title and thumbnail promised in the first seconds (MrBeast buried alive: first shot = the thumbnail, first sentence = the title); (2) context: why this matters, only as much as needed; (3) curiosity: a question that cannot be answered without watching (blur the five steps; show the best scene and cut just before the climax; MrBeast teases five traps in 12 s = five gaps); (4) a differentiator: why this video and not another (authority: "tens of millions by 22"; effort or money: "I'm about to spend $20,000 in the next 5 minutes"); (5) sound design: the music sets the feeling, and every item on screen gets its own sound (a cash-register ring with each price).
- The order of 2–4 is free; never promise more than the video gives.

### T10 Alex Price-Hunt, "Steal these 5 visual hooks" (Short, 16 s, 1.71M views, 35,918 likes, 18 Jan 2025; watched)
- quick appearance (starts mid-motion, face and hands already moving) · fast cuts (0.2–0.3 s each, a foley sound on every cut) · walk-in (a blurred wide shot snaps into focus while the subject comes toward the camera) · transition hook (a bizarre image + a stomp that shakes the screen, with an impact sound) · match cut (the same lines carry over into the next scene).
- For a 3D world: start mid-move; a fast push-in from blur; 3–4 flash cuts in the first second; something heavy lands and the frame shakes; one shape turning into another (a coin into the sun, a chart line into a mountain ridge).
